Contest

Top prizes, 1st place first. Leave trailing boxes blank to use fewer places. A tie for 1st among g entries pays the average of places 1 through g.

Advanced: salary decay and chalk correlation

Most of a large field spends $49,500 to $50,000. A lineup that leaves money unspent is built by fewer people, so its duplicate chance is multiplied by e-remaining / decay. At the default $1,500, leaving $1,500 unspent cuts the estimate to 37%. A larger decay means a smaller penalty.

The base estimate treats every player pick as independent. Real entries are not: people stack a quarterback with his receivers and pair popular plays, so chalk combinations show up together more often than the product of ownerships suggests. Values above 1 raise the duplicate estimate for that effect. 1.0 means no adjustment.

      Method and limits
      

        
This is a heuristic estimator. It does not measure real contest entries; it turns projected ownership into a rough duplicate count.

        
1. Base match chance. Treat each opponent entry as picking each of your nine players independently, with chance equal to that player's projected ownership.

        p0 = own_1 x own_2 x ... x own_9      (ownership as fractions)
        
A Classic lineup is a set of nine players. Two entries with the same nine players are the same lineup even if two receivers sit in different WR slots or a different RB fills FLEX, and ownership already counts a player in whatever slot he was used. So no permutation count is multiplied in; doing so would double count.

        
2. Salary left on the table. s = exp(-remaining / decay), decay $1,500 by default. A full-cap lineup gets s = 1.

        
3. Chalk correlation. c, 0.5 to 5, default 1.0. A plain multiplier for stacking and other correlated picks.

        p = min(1, p0 x s x c)
E = (N - 1) x p
P(unique) = (1 - p)^(N - 1)
P(k) = C(N-1, k) p^k (1 - p)^(N-1-k)     k = 0, 1, 2, ...
        
The binomial is computed exactly in log space. The Poisson approximation with mean E is shown beside it; for large fields the two differ by at most (N - 1) p2 in total variation (Le Cam's bound), which is tiny here.

        
4. Tie split. If your lineup finishes first and k others hold it, the k+1 tied entries split places 1 through k+1, so each gets their average. Places beyond the prizes you entered are valued at your last entered prize.

        E[prize | you finish 1st] = sum over k of P(k) x avg(prize_1 ... prize_k+1)
        
Limits

        
    
          
  • Ownership projections are themselves estimates, and the result is very sensitive to them: nine numbers are multiplied, so a 20% error in each compounds to about a factor of 5 when they all run high (1.2^9) and about 7 when they all run low (0.8^9).
    • 
          
  • Independence is wrong for real fields. Stacks, game environments and popular optimizer settings cluster entries. The correlation multiplier is a single knob, not a model of that structure.
    • 
          
  • Ownership values are treated as independent draws even though a real roster cannot repeat a position past its slot count. For typical ownership levels the effect is small next to the projection error.
    • 
          
  • Multi-entry players can enter the same lineup many times. Those copies are not independent and are not modeled.
    • 
          
  • The salary factor is a smooth guess at how the field spends salary. It is not fit to any contest data.
    • 
          
  • The payout table ignores the rest of the prize structure; only the places a tie for first can reach matter for the number shown.
    • 
          
  • Results inform contest strategy only. They are not a forecast of winnings.
    •
